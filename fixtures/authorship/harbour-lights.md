# Harbour lights 🗼

A field guide written by several people and two machines, for testing how a file keeps track of who wrote what. Nothing here is true unless someone checked it.

## Why a harbour needs a light

Ships arrive at night more often than anyone would like. A harbour light is less about brightness than about **character**: the pattern of flashes and eclipses that tells a navigator *which* light she is looking at. Two white flashes every ten seconds means one headland; a steady green means the end of a breakwater. Get the pattern wrong and a ship turns into rocks with complete confidence. ⚓️

The oldest lights were fires in iron baskets. Coal burned well and smoked badly. Oil lamps came next, then clockwork to turn a lens, then electricity, then nobody living at the light at all.

> The keeper's log for the third of March records "wind backing west, glass falling, lamp lit at 17:42, three ships passed inward" and nothing else, which is what a good log looks like.

## How the lens works

A Fresnel lens is a flat lens cut into rings. Each ring bends light by the amount a much thicker lens would have, so the whole thing is lighter and loses less light in the glass. The rings are arranged so that light leaving the lamp in every direction is folded into a single horizontal sheet. Turn the lens and the sheet sweeps the horizon; the sweep is what a ship sees as a flash.

- First-order lenses stand taller than a person.
- Sixth-order lenses fit on a kitchen table.
- Café owners near the harbour sell postcards of both. ☕️

Some keepers wrote poems. Most wrote shopping lists: paraffin, wicks, flour, tea, a new brush for the brass. The brass was always being polished.

## The keeper's family

Children who grew up at a light remember the weather more than the work. One remembered counting the steps — 217 of them — every morning before school, and that the cat, a large ginger tom named Fresnel 🐈, refused to climb past the 100th.

Another remembered the fog signal: a horn that sounded every thirty seconds for as long as the fog lasted, sometimes three days. “You stop hearing it,” she said, “until it stops, and then the silence wakes you.”

Families planted gardens in tins and old boots, because the soil on a rock is a handful at best. Rosemary did well. Tomatoes did not. 🍅

## Automation

By the end of the last century most lights ran themselves. A sensor decides when it is dark, a motor turns the lens or a LED array flashes on a timer, and a radio reports faults to an office on land. The keepers' houses became holiday lets and museums, and the brass went dull.

Automation is reliable and dull, which is the point. A light that needs no one is a light that is always on. Still, some harbours keep a volunteer who climbs up once a week, “just to see,” and writes a line in the log that nobody is required to read.

## Notes from the archive

The following were copied from a ledger by a volunteer who was not sure about the spelling of several names:

1. *Naïve* drawings of the lamp room, in pencil, with the date written backwards.
2. A list of ships, some named for saints and some for wives: *Saint Ninian*, *Margaret Ann*, *Ingrid*, *Hope*.
3. A recipe for a cleaning paste using whiting, ammonia and “elbow grease, a great deal.”
4. A note in another hand: 灯台守の日記 — a keeper's diary, in Japanese, which nobody at the archive could read until a visitor did.

The visitor translated one line: “The sea was quiet. I painted the door again.” 🖌️
